                 REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE




2.1 Introduction to the Review
         This chapter reviews two bodies of research: the program-analysis and
command-safety work on which the study’s recoverability analysis builds, and the
work on conversational and language-model-mediated interfaces to personal computing

in which SynapseOS, the platform where the analysis is deployed, is situated. The
review spans nearly four decades of work on natural-language interfaces to computing
systems, from the symbolic-artificial-intelligence consultants developed for the Unix
shell in the late 1980s [45] to the large language model (LLM) agents that now operate
shells, graphical applications, browsers, and mobile devices. Its purpose is threefold:
to establish the conceptual vocabulary and technical foundations on which the present

study builds, to characterize the current state of the field and its recurring limitations,
and to locate precisely the gaps that the proposed recoverability analysis, and
SynapseOS, the system in which it is deployed, are designed to fill.



2.2 Theoretical Framework

         This review rests on two frameworks of different standing. The first is the
theoretical basis of the study’s contribution: the program-analysis tradition in which the
recoverability analysis is an instance. The second organizes the literature on language-
mediated interfaces, in which the platform that deploys the analysis is positioned.

Program analysis: abstract interpretation and effect systems
        The technique the study’s recoverability analysis relies on — determining

what a piece of code will do without executing it — is not new; it has a decades-long
history in programming-language theory under two related names, neither of which the
natural-language shells and command guards surveyed in this chapter draw on.
Abstract interpretation, introduced by Cousot and Cousot [6], is the foundational
technique for computing sound, conservative approximations of a program’s runtime
behavior by evaluating it over an abstract domain rather than its concrete one. Type-

and-effect systems, developed by Jouvelot and Gifford [18] and given a full formal
treatment by Talpin and Jouvelot [40], extend a program’s type judgment with an
explicit account of the side effects an expression may produce, so that a compiler or
static checker can reason about those effects instead of leaving them implicit in
execution. The recoverability algorithm this study contributes (Chapter 3, Section
2.1(a)–(c)) is an instance of this discipline applied to a domain neither tradition has
targeted: a generated shell command is treated as an expression whose effect — on the
filesystem, rather than on a type system’s abstract state — is derived from its syntax

and composed across its operators, and it is that effect set, not the command’s name,
that the recoverability verdict is computed over. This connects the algorithm to an
established formal lineage rather than presenting it as a bespoke heuristic, and it
grounds the algorithm’s one-sided soundness property — over-approximating
destructiveness under uncertainty rather than under-approximating it — in the same
requirement abstract interpretation itself imposes: a sound abstraction may lose
precision, but it may never assert a property the concrete execution does not actually
have.
        Three properties of the analysis follow from this framework and are carried
through the rest of the study. Its abstract domain is the effect set: what a command

would read, create, write, remove, or alter in metadata, rather than the command’s
name. Its semantics is compositional: the effects of a line are built from the effects of
its parts through the operators that join them, which is what an enumerated list of

                                                                                           18


command patterns cannot express. And its soundness is one-sided: whatever cannot be
determined is treated as unrecoverable, so that the analysis may ask more often than
necessary but may never declare a destructive command safe.



The four-layer interaction pipeline
          The heterogeneous systems surveyed in this chapter — shell translators, web
and mobile agents, desktop automation frameworks, and accessibility interfaces —
differ widely in platform, action space, and evaluation method, yet they address a
common problem: converting a user’s expressed intent into effects on a computing
system.    To compare them on common ground, this review adopts a four-layer
interaction pipeline — Input, Reasoning, Action, and Operating-System Integration —

as its framework for the interface literature. The framework is adapted from the
layered decomposition of large language model (LLM)-brained graphical user interface
(GUI) agents proposed in the survey of Zhang and colleagues [49], generalized here
from graphical agents specifically to language-mediated computing interfaces as a
class. Figure 2.1 depicts the pipeline and situates the layers relative to one another.

        This framework is appropriate to that literature for three reasons. First, it is

modality-neutral: because it abstracts over whether intent arrives as text, speech, or
gesture and whether action is realized through shell commands, API calls, or simulated
clicks, it accommodates the full span of surveyed systems without privileging any one
interaction style — a necessary property for a review that spans command-line,
graphical, and accessibility work. Second, it is diagnostic: locating each system’s
principal limitation at a specific layer — visual grounding as a Reasoning-and-Action
failure, or narrow platform coverage as an Operating-System Integration limitation —
turns an otherwise flat catalogue of systems into a structured comparison, and it is this
layer-by-layer positioning that the comparative synthesis of §2.9 makes explicit. Third,
it is directly applicable to the platform: SynapseOS, in which the analysis is deployed
(Chapter 3, Figure 3.1), instantiates this same pipeline — accepting natural-language

intent at the Input layer, parsing it with a locally-hosted small language model at the
Reasoning layer, translating it into shell commands executed through the standard
toolchain at the Action layer, and spanning the full graphical session rather than a
single application at the Operating-System Integration layer. The pipeline therefore
serves both as the organizing lens for the interface literature and as the vocabulary in
which the platform is later positioned; the analysis sits between its Reasoning and
Action layers, where a generated command is about to take effect.

LLM-based reasoning; they address speech- or accessibility-driven interaction with

2.3 Historical Foundations of Natural-Language Interfaces
        The aspiration to let users describe what they want in ordinary language and
have a computer carry it out is not new. The earliest serious system in this tradition,
the Berkeley Unix Consultant of Wilensky and colleagues [45], was already operational
by the late 1980s. The Unix Consultant was an intelligent natural-language interface
that allowed naive users to learn about the Unix operating system through conversation
in ordinary English, addressing both the natural-language-frontend problem and the

consultation problem, and thereby distinguishing itself from systems intended only as
command translators. It was built on the KODIAK knowledge representation and
incorporated a user-modeling component that represented the user’s knowledge state
and a teaching component through which users could extend its knowledge base in
natural language. The system demonstrated end-to-end natural-language consultation
about Unix, successfully answering "how do I," definitional, and problem-solving
questions.   Critically, its authors identified knowledge acquisition as the principal
bottleneck: extending the system to a new domain required substantial hand-
engineering — a concern that directly foreshadows the contemporary problem of LLM-
based agents requiring per-application grounding data.
        The intervening decades saw natural-language interaction treated primarily as

a supervised-learning problem. Representative of this pre-LLM era, the work of Li and
colleagues on mapping natural-language instructions to mobile user-interface action
sequences [20] trained dedicated phrase-extraction and grounding transformers on
hand-annotated action sequences, achieving 70.59% accuracy on predicting complete
ground-truth action sequences on its benchmark but remaining bound to its purpose-

──────── p. 22 ────────
                                                                                       22


built datasets and architectures. The decisive shift came with the introduction of in-
context reasoning patterns for LLMs. ReAct, introduced by Yao and colleagues [47],
collapsed the multi-stage supervised pipeline into a prompting strategy: the language
model is prompted to alternate between free-text "Thought" steps that update its plan
and "Action" steps that interact with an external environment, with observations fed
back into subsequent reasoning. Implemented purely as a prompting strategy without
fine-tuning, ReAct nonetheless outperformed imitation-learning and reinforcement-
learning baselines that required thousands of training trajectories.

         The historical significance of this transition is twofold, and it frames the
remainder of this review. First, it explains why progress in the field has been so rapid
since 2023: the methodological foundations became portable across modalities and
platforms in a way that the earlier dataset-bound approaches were not. Nearly every
system surveyed in the sections that follow inherits from ReAct the convention of
interleaving reasoning traces with environment-affecting actions. Second, this shared
inheritance underscores a corresponding fragility: these systems inherit the failure
modes of LLM reasoning — hallucinated actions, plan inconsistency, and brittleness on
long-horizon tasks — alongside their capabilities, a concern that the evaluation
literature reviewed in §2.8 quantifies.



Interface paradigms and the case against natural language
         The aspiration to a natural-language interface has a long-standing critique that
remains unanswered, and a review that omitted it would misrepresent the state of the
question. Shneiderman’s account of direct manipulation [37] holds that interfaces
succeed when the objects of interest are continuously visible, when actions upon them

are rapid, incremental, and reversible, and when their effects are immediately apparent
— properties that let a user build and maintain an accurate model of system state.
Norman formalized what an interface does for a user as the bridging of two gulfs [28]:
the gulf of execution, between an intention and the actions a system makes available,

──────── p. 23 ────────
                                                                                         23


and the gulf of evaluation, between the system’s response and the user’s ability to
interpret it.   Hutchins, Hollan, and Norman [16] argued that direct manipulation
narrows both, by making an interface feel like the object of interest itself rather than a
description of it.
         A language interface sits awkwardly against nearly every one of those
properties. An utterance is not continuously visible, not incremental, and not reversible
by the same gesture that produced it; the set of available actions is not displayed, so the
user must guess at the system’s capabilities rather than see them. Shneiderman and
Maes debated precisely this trade-off between direct manipulation and interface agents

[39], and Shneiderman argued subsequently that speech and language interaction
consume the same cognitive resources a user needs in order to hold a task in mind,
where pointing does not [38]. Empirical work on modern conversational agents has
largely borne the concern out: Luger and Sellen documented a persistent gulf between
what users expected a conversational agent to understand and what it actually did [24],
and Clark and colleagues found that the properties people value in human conversation
transfer poorly to interaction with an agent [5].
         This critique is the strongest argument against the premise of the present
study, and it shapes the system’s design rather than being set aside by it. Two of its
three concerns are addressed directly. The gulf of evaluation is narrowed by displaying

the generated command and its raw output rather than reporting an outcome in prose,
so that the user sees what was actually done rather than a description of it. The
irreversibility of an utterance is answered by the confirmation gate and the undo
journal, which restore by mechanism the reversibility that direct manipulation obtains
for free. The third concern — that a user cannot see the boundary of what the system is
able to do — is not solved by this design and is carried forward as an acknowledged
limitation.
         The comparative question is older than the current literature and considerably
less settled than it is often assumed to be. It is widely held that the command line’s
power is inaccessible to most people in practice.        But the best-known controlled

──────── p. 24 ────────
                                                                                        24


comparison of command, menu, and iconic interfaces, conducted by Whiteside and
colleagues across seventy-six users and seven systems [44], concluded that interface
style itself did not predict either performance or preference: careful design did, and the
system that served novices best also served experts best. That finding is as much a
caution for the present study as a motivation for it. It warns against attributing to a
conversational paradigm what may in fact belong to the quality of one particular
implementation, and it is a principal reason this study measures a specific system
against a specific baseline rather than claiming to measure one paradigm against
another.



2.4 Natural-Language-to-Shell Translation
           The thread of the literature most directly related to the proposed system
concerns the translation of natural language into shell commands. Because SynapseOS
grounds user intent into commands executed through the standard Linux command-line

toolchain, the accuracy, safety, and context-awareness of natural-language-to-Bash
translation are of central relevance to its design.
           Westenfelder and colleagues [43] address this translation problem directly.
Their work constructs a manually verified test set of 600 instruction–command pairs
and a training set of 40,939 pairs, and introduces a functional-equivalence heuristic that
reaches 95% confidence in determining whether two Bash commands produce the
same effect — allowing automated evaluation to move beyond exact-string matching to
a measure of whether a generated command actually accomplishes the intended task.
Their fine-tuned models substantially outperform zero-shot baselines, demonstrating
that fine-tuning specifically on command-translation data is effective. The authors
identify two persistent limitations: ambiguity in user instructions, and the absence of

system context — the contents of the current directory, the user’s command history,

──────── p. 25 ────────
                                                                                         25


and environment variables — at translation time. Integrating context-aware retrieval is
identified as a direction, one that the persistent conversational session of SynapseOS is
well positioned to supply.
            The feasibility of deploying such a system without reliance on a hosted
frontier model is supported by the large-scale evaluation of Jacobs and colleagues [17],
who assess a broad range of locally deployable open-source LLMs on Bash command
generation. Their work provides an empirical anchor for the viability of running
natural-language-to-Bash translation on local, open-weights models of modest size —
the deployment posture adopted by SynapseOS for reasons of privacy and offline
operation. Taken together, the shell-and-command-line thread establishes that natural-

language-to-Bash translation is tractable, that safety and reversibility are central rather
than incidental concerns, and that local deployment is feasible; yet no surveyed shell-
level system has been evaluated with users against the conventional workflow it would
supplement. How such systems guard the commands they generate, and what static
analysis of shell commands can establish, are taken up in §2.5.



2.5 Command Safety, Static Analysis, and Recovery
            The literature closest to the study’s algorithm concerns how shell commands
are checked before they run. It falls into three groups: guards, which decide whether a
generated command may run; analyses, which reason about what a shell program does;
and recovery mechanisms, which restore a system after the fact. Each establishes part
of what the study needs, and none, as this section shows, determines before a command
runs whether its effects can be undone and captures exactly what undoing them
requires.

                                                                                      26


Guards
         Where translation accuracy is the concern of Westenfelder and colleagues
(§2.4), safety is the concern of Gyawali and colleagues, whose NaSh system [11]
proposes guardrails for an LLM-powered natural-language shell for Linux. Published
under the operating-systems classification, NaSh interposes a guardrail layer between
the user’s natural-language input and the LLM-generated command: the model
proposes a candidate command, the guardrail layer evaluates it against safety,
reversibility, and confirmation criteria, and the user receives a structured preview
before execution.     The authors note that LLM outputs may be unintended or
unexplainable, requiring guardrails for user error recovery, and they leave as open work

both the design of recovery and rollback mechanisms for irreversible system operations
and the empirical user evaluation of the approach. The first of these open problems,
recovery from an operation already approved, is the one the present study’s
recoverability analysis addresses; the second is taken up by its validation in use.
         The same design is what ships.         Coding agents that execute generated
commands guard them with lists of command patterns: the dangerous-command check
of OpenAI’s Codex CLI [29] and cc-safety-net [19], a pre-execution guard used with
several agentic coding tools, are representative open-source examples, and both are
scored as baselines in Chapter 3, Section 3.1(a). Both are designed for agents working
inside a version-controlled project, where deleting project files is routine and version
control, rather than the guard, is what makes a mistake recoverable. A personal

computer’s files have no such safeguard, which is the setting of this study.
         The gap these guards share is the one this study’s algorithmic contribution
addresses.   Every surveyed system that guards a generated command does so by
permission: the command is matched against an enumerated set of patterns judged
dangerous, and is then allowed, refused, or escalated to the user. NaSh [11] proposes
guardrails of this kind and, notably, identifies selective undo as a requirement while
leaving its mechanism open; the shipped guards above operate the same way in
practice, with allow-and-deny lists over command prefixes.          A permission model

──────── p. 27 ────────
                                                                                         27


answers whether a command may run. It does not answer whether, having run, the
command can be undone — and those are different questions with different answers.
Refusing a command protects a user only until they approve it, which for any system
that must let work proceed is most of the time.
         The distinction matters because enumeration does not compose. A pattern list
matches a command in isolation, whereas a generated line routinely chains several
operations whose recovery requirements differ — a removal, a version-control reset,
and an in-place edit may appear in one invocation joined by &&, with redirects and
command substitution altering what each actually touches.            No enumeration of
command prefixes expresses that, and extending the list does not converge: each newly
named tool leaves the next one unnamed. Determining recoverability over a composed

command, and computing the minimal information needed to reverse it, is the open
problem this study takes up (RQ1–RQ3), and it is what separates the present work from
the guardrail designs surveyed above. The comparison that matters is not only against
an enumerated list of dangerous commands. A list can be turned around so that it
refuses every command it does not recognize, which removes the first weakness above
at the cost of asking about everything unfamiliar; that fail-closed list is the strongest
simple baseline, and the study therefore claims what the analysis adds to it — seeing
through wrappers, resolving run-time targets, and capturing exactly what would be lost
— and not the verdict alone.



Static analysis of shell programs
         Static analysis of shell scripts specifically is comparatively little studied, and
the existing work targets properties adjacent to recoverability rather than recoverability
itself. ABash [25] performs abstract interpretation over bash’s variable-expansion
semantics to catch common expansion bugs and includes taint tracking, but is not
concerned with what a script’s commands would do to the filesystem if executed

correctly. Smoosh [10] gives the POSIX shell a mechanized, executable small-step

──────── p. 28 ────────
                                                                                         28


operational semantics, validated against other shells' conformance test suites — exactly

the kind of ground-truth semantics a static effect analysis would need to be checked
against — but stops at defining that semantics rather than analyzing programs written
in it. Closest of the three is CoLiS [1], which applies symbolic execution to the more
than twenty-seven thousand POSIX-shell maintainer scripts in the Debian package
archive to verify idempotence — that running a script a second time causes no further
harm — a property Debian policy requires of these scripts because they run with root
privileges during install, upgrade, and removal. Idempotence and recoverability are
related but distinct: a script can be idempotent while still being destructive on its first
run — rm -rf /var/cache/pkg is idempotent, since running it twice does no more
damage than once, and unrecoverable, since the first run already destroyed what a
recovery would need — and a command can be recoverable without being idempotent,

if each run produces a new, independently-reversible effect. CoLiS is therefore the
nearest prior application of static effect reasoning to shell scripts specifically, and it
stops short of the present study’s question for exactly this reason: verifying that re-
execution is safe does not determine whether the first execution can be undone. No
work identified in this review derives a recoverability verdict for a shell command
from its statically-computed effects; that is the specific gap the present study’s
algorithmic contribution fills.



Recovery after the fact
         Guards and analyses act before a command runs. A separate literature restores
a system after something has gone wrong, and it approaches the study’s question from
the other side: not what a command will destroy, but how to recover what was
destroyed. Three kinds of mechanism are relevant.
         Filesystem snapshots are the most widely deployed.             A copy-on-write
filesystem such as btrfs can snapshot a whole subvolume at little cost, because a

snapshot shares its blocks with the live filesystem until they are modified [34], so

──────── p. 29 ────────
                                                                                        29


snapshots can be taken on a schedule or before system updates. A snapshot needs no
knowledge of the command it protects against, which is both its strength and its limit.

Restoring one returns the whole subvolume to an earlier moment and discards every
later change along with the unwanted one, and nothing about a snapshot can tell the
user, before a command runs, whether that command will lose anything.              On a
filesystem without copy-on-write, such as the ext4 on which this study is conducted, a
snapshot must visit every file in the tree, a cost measured in Chapter 3, Section 3.1(a).
Versioning file systems instead keep old versions of individual files: the Elephant file
system retains versions under per-file policies that decide when an old version may be
forgotten [35]. Recovery is then per file and needs no foresight, but its storage cost is
paid on every change, mistaken or not, and obtaining it means replacing the filesystem.
         Undo at the level of a service or the operating system makes recovery a

property of the system’s design.        Operator Undo intercepts and logs the user
interactions with an e-mail service before they reach it, so that an operator can rewind
the service’s state, repair it, and replay what users did since [4]. Operating system
transactions let a program group system calls so that their effects can be rolled back as
a unit before they commit [32]. Intrusion recovery systems such as Taser log system
activity so that the effects of an attack can be identified and reverted afterward while
legitimate changes are kept [9]. Each restores more completely than any list can, but
each requires something the setting of this study does not have: a service built to be
undoable, a kernel and programs that use transactions, or continuous logging of all
system activity.
         The present study takes a narrower path between these literatures. Like a

guard, it acts on one command before the command runs, on an unmodified system and
filesystem. Like a recovery mechanism, it restores. It captures only what that one
command would destroy, which it determines by analysis, so its cost follows from what
the command touches rather than from the size of the system, and it can say before the
command runs whether undo will be possible. Table 2.1 compares the approaches
surveyed in this section on these properties.

2.6 Graphical and Computer-Using Agents
         A parallel and larger body of work addresses computer-using agents1 —

systems that operate graphical environments by issuing actions and observing results.
Although SynapseOS operates at the command line rather than by simulating graphical
interaction, this literature is essential context: it establishes the dominant architectural
patterns, defines the recurring technical bottleneck of visual grounding2, and, through

──────── p. 31 ────────
                                                                                      31


its platform coverage, reveals the gap that motivates the present study. The work is
organized here by the platform each system targets — web, mobile, and desktop —
together with the vision-language foundation models on which many depend.



Web agents
         The web has been the most heavily studied environment for language-guided
agents. Mind2Web, introduced by Deng and colleagues [8], is the first dataset for
developing generalist web agents that follow language instructions across diverse real-

world websites, comprising over two thousand open-ended tasks collected from 137
websites across 31 domains; its accompanying two-stage model uses a small language
model to filter candidate elements from large documents before invoking a larger
model to choose among them. WebArena, introduced by Zhou and colleagues [53],
provides a fully self-hosted, reproducible web environment of functional website
clones and evaluates 812 long-horizon tasks for functional correctness rather than
trajectory similarity, establishing a challenging benchmark on which contemporary
models achieved completion rates far below human performance.               Zheng and
colleagues [52], in their SeeAct framework, decouple visual understanding from action:
the model observes a screenshot, generates a textual plan, and a separate step grounds

that plan onto page elements. Their central finding is diagnostic for the entire field —
with oracle grounding provided, the model completes 51.1% of tasks on live websites,
substantially outperforming a text-only baseline at 13.3%, but performance degrades
sharply when grounding must itself be inferred, confirming visual grounding as the
principal bottleneck.



Mobile agents
         Mobile-device control has produced large-scale datasets and progressively
more autonomous agents. The pre-LLM work of Li and colleagues [20], discussed in
§2.3, established the grounding problem for mobile interfaces. Android in the Wild,

──────── p. 32 ────────
                                                                                       32


from Rawles and colleagues [33], contributes a dataset of 715,000 episodes spanning
30,000 unique instructions, in which actions are gestures at arbitrary screen coordinates
rather than operations on indexed interface elements, exposing the challenge that

actions must be inferred from visual appearance rather than from a structured model of
the interface.   AppAgent, from Zhang and colleagues [51], operates Android
applications through a simplified action space of taps and swipes and learns to use new
applications through autonomous exploration or by observing human demonstrations,
generating application-specific documentation that matched or exceeded human-written
documentation. Mobile-Agent, from Wang and colleagues [42], adopts a purely vision-
centric approach that requires no system metadata, using optical-character-recognition
and icon-detection modules to locate interface elements — achieving broad
applicability across mobile environments at the cost of vision-grounding errors that
require recovery mechanisms.



Desktop and cross-application agents
        Desktop agents are the most direct graphical analogue to the present work, and
their limitations are the most instructive. ScreenAgent, from Niu and colleagues [27],
is a vision-language-model-driven agent for cross-application desktop control that
operates in a plan–act–reflect cycle, specifying actions as coordinates and key

sequences; its authors acknowledge that vision-only grounding is less reliable than
approaches with API access.       WinClick, from Hui and colleagues [15], targets
Windows-desktop grounding and observes that most existing GUI agents rely on
structured data formats such as the document object model or HyperText Markup
Language, which are unavailable across general desktop environments; it therefore
performs visual grounding directly from screenshots. UFO², the "Desktop AgentOS"
of Zhang and colleagues [50], is the most ambitious desktop system surveyed: it fuses
the native Windows accessibility framework with vision-based parsing in a hybrid
action mechanism, dispatching subtasks from a coordinating agent to application-

──────── p. 33 ────────
                                                                                        33


specialized agents. Its authors argue that existing computer-using agents are hindered
by shallow operating-system integration, fragile screenshot-based interaction, and
disruptive execution, and they identify adaptation to other desktop platforms, such as
Linux and macOS, as future work. UFO² nonetheless operates as an automation layer

dispatched atop the existing Windows shell rather than as a replacement for it, and its
authors frame cross-platform deployment as groundwork for a unified ecosystem of
desktop automation solutions — the trajectory from which the present study departs.



Contemporaneous commercial systems (as of September 2026)

         The systems surveyed above are research artifacts. A parallel commercial
development is moving faster than the peer-reviewed literature and warrants explicit
positioning; what follows reflects that landscape as of September 2026 and is dated
deliberately, because it will continue to move.        Several products now describe
themselves as agentic operating systems. eva AG’s WarmWind, released in 2026, runs
autonomous workers that operate applications through vision and simulated mouse and
keyboard input, hosted in a managed cloud environment built on a Linux base and
aimed at automating repetitive office work. Alibaba’s ANOLISA ships an AI-native
Linux terminal in which shell commands and ordinary language share a single prompt,
with the agent requesting approval before risky operations and a checkpoint facility
providing rollback. Major platform vendors have each shipped or previewed some

form of computer control, ranging from screenshot-driven graphical automation to
structured application-intent dispatch. Two properties separate all of them from the
present work, and both are structural rather than incidental. First, each routes inference
through remote infrastructure, whereas the design constraint here is that the model runs
on the user’s own hardware, with no network dependency. Second, each is built to act
on the user’s behalf, largely unattended, whereas the interface studied here is operated
by a person turn by turn with every irreversible action gated on that person’s approval.

──────── p. 34 ────────
                                                                                        34


The distinction is not which approach is better but which question is being asked: these
systems pursue autonomy, while this study asks whether conversation is a better
interface for a human who is present and in control.



Vision-language foundation models
          Underlying many of these agents are vision-language models trained
specifically for interface understanding. CogAgent, from Hong and colleagues [14], is
an   eighteen-billion-parameter    visual   language    model    specialized   for   GUI
understanding and navigation, using both low- and high-resolution image encoders to
recognize the small icons and text typical of graphical interfaces. It supports the

position that pure-vision GUI agents are viable when the underlying model is trained
appropriately — but at a compute cost, and with the cross-platform generalization
limitations common to the entire graphical-agent literature. Across web, mobile, and
desktop, these systems demonstrate broad capability within a single platform class,
while consistently identifying visual grounding as an unsolved bottleneck and trending
toward hybrid execution that prefers structured access where available and falls back to
vision.



2.7 Accessibility and Speech-Driven Interaction
          A distinct thread of the literature approaches conversational computing from
the perspective of accessibility, targeting users with motor, speech, or cognitive needs.
This thread is relevant to the present study in two ways: it provides the most rigorous
human-centered evaluations in the field, and it exhibits a limitation that is the mirror
image of the mainstream agent literature, sharpening the opportunity the proposed
system addresses.

          VoicePilot, from Padmanabha and colleagues [30], is a framework for using
LLMs as speech interfaces to physically assistive robots for individuals with motor
impairments. Although its immediate target is robotic assistance rather than desktop

──────── p. 35 ────────
                                                                                          35


computing, it is included here because it is the most rigorous human-centered
evaluation in the LLM-speech-interface literature. The authors derive five concrete
design guidelines from a study with motor-impaired participants, among them the
importance of execution speed approaching that of a caregiver and the necessity of
preview-and-confirm workflows — a guideline that directly informs the reversibility-

based confirmation design of the present study.         DexAssist, from Mehendale and
Walishetti [26], targets web navigation by users with fine-motor impairments using two
LLMs in tandem — one to interpret voice commands and a second to map the
interpretation onto interface elements with fallback strategies — reducing the
misinterpretation failures of single-model voice navigation on dense layouts. The
architecture of van Dam [41] proposes retrofitting LLM-based conversational
interfaces onto existing single-application graphical interfaces, exposing an
application’s navigation graph through a tool protocol and handling voice input through
real-time speech recognition; its author observes that most production applications
were never designed with speech in mind, making such retrofitting an ongoing
challenge.

         More recent work continues to mature the components required for speech-
driven interface control. Han and colleagues, in GUIRoboTron-Speech [12], advance
automated GUI agents driven directly by speech instructions, while Park and
colleagues, in R-VLM [31], contribute region-aware visual grounding for more precise
identification of on-screen elements. What unites this thread is that it operates at the
level of the individual application or interaction technique rather than at the level of the
session itself.   These approaches presuppose an underlying conversational system
surface on which to run, yet no such surface exists at the session level.              This
complementarity is significant: a general-purpose conversational interface layer would
serve not only mainstream users but also provide the substrate that accessibility-

specific configurations require — positioning accessibility not as the immediate target
of the present study, which scopes it out for recruitment and ethics reasons, but as a
natural and well-grounded extension of its contribution.

2.8 Evaluation Environments and Benchmarks
        The rapid proliferation of computer-using agents has been accompanied by
increasingly rigorous evaluation environments, and these establish both the current
ceiling of the field’s capability and the methods by which that capability is measured.
The most consequential is OSWorld, from Xie and colleagues [46], a benchmark of 369
real-world computer tasks in a real-computer environment spanning Ubuntu, Windows,

and macOS. At its 2024 release the margin it measured was stark: humans achieved
72.36% success where the best evaluated model reached 12.24%. That particular
margin has since closed, with leading agents now exceeding the original human
baseline on the revised task set — but the closure reflects progress on short, self-
contained tasks rather than on sustained computer work. Its successor, OSWorld 2.0
[48], evaluates 108 long-horizon workflows whose median item requires roughly an
hour and a half of skilled human operation; there the best frontier configuration
completes only 20.6% of tasks outright. The reported failures concentrate not in
interface navigation but in tracking constraints across a long task, absorbing
information that arrives mid-way, and verifying a result before declaring completion.

The field’s ceiling has moved substantially; it has not been reached. OSWorld is also
directly relevant to the present study as the source of a real-task suite against which
benchmark comparability can be established.
        Complementing the benchmark literature, the survey of LLM-brained GUI
agents by Zhang and colleagues [49] synthesizes the field through 2024, organizing it
around the components of perception, planning, action, and memory, and providing the
four-layer vocabulary adopted in §2.2 of this review. It enumerates the field’s open
challenges — accurate element localization, knowledge retrieval, long-horizon
planning, and safety-aware execution control; notably, none of the challenges it
enumerates concerns replacing the session layer itself: the survey takes the surrounding
operating-system session as given, which is the assumption the present study sets out to

test. What the evaluation literature reveals, however, is a specific methodological
limitation: rigorous as these benchmarks are, they measure the autonomous task

──────── p. 37 ────────
                                                                                              37


completion of agents acting on a user’s behalf, not the comparative performance of a
human user working through a conversational interface versus a conventional graphical
one. That distinction motivates the evaluation design reviewed in §2.10.

restores wholesale or needs a system built for it, and none derives from a command’s

           Six patterns emerge from this comparison, each surfacing a recurring theme in
the literature.



Bifurcation by coverage scope
           The field is bifurcated by coverage scope. Shell-level work [11, 43] and
single-application work [30, 41] sit at the narrow end; cross-application desktop, web,
and mobile agents sit at the broad end but each within a single platform class. No
surveyed system implements a working conversational layer over the full graphical
session that has been empirically validated against the conventional desktop workflow

it would supplement. The Unix Consultant [45] came closest in ambition four decades
ago, but its pre-LLM foundation prevented it from scaling beyond demonstration. The
absence is structural rather than incidental. Even UFO² [50], the most operationally
advanced desktop-agent system surveyed, is by construction an automation layer

──────── p. 40 ────────
                                                                                         40


dispatched atop the existing Windows shell, and its stated future direction is broader
cross-platform automation rather than replacement of the session itself.               The
substitution this study attempts is therefore unclaimed, not merely under-published.



Execution mechanism at the action layer
            A second pattern concerns the execution mechanism at the Action layer. The
literature divides among command translation, API binding, and GUI simulation, with
a dominant trend toward hybrid execution [41, 50] that prefers structured access where
available and falls back to vision.       Vision-only systems [27, 42] achieve broad
applicability at the cost of grounding fragility, while structured-access systems achieve
precision at the cost of per-environment integration. Notably, the Unix Consultant
operated purely at the consultation layer without executing actions — an approach that
reflects an underappreciated design principle, that the agent that explains can be safer

than the agent that acts, which the confirmation-gated design of the present study
revisits.



Visual grounding as the principal bottleneck
            A third pattern is that visual grounding is the field’s principal bottleneck,

identified explicitly by both SeeAct [52] and the GUI-agents survey [49]. The 51.1%
ceiling of GPT-4V with oracle grounding and its sharp degradation without are
attributable in significant part to grounding failure. This bottleneck is a property of the
graphical-simulation approach specifically; a command-line approach that grounds
intent into shell commands rather than into on-screen pixel regions sidesteps it, at the
cost of being bounded by what is expressible on the command line. Direct comparison
qualifies that tradeoff usefully.     Across 440 matched desktop tasks, screen-only
graphical agents pass 59.1% while command-line agents reach only 48.2% — the shell
is not automatically the stronger substrate — but command-line agents augmented with
result verification reach 69.3%, the highest of the three conditions [54]. Execution

──────── p. 41 ────────
                                                                                      41


through the shell becomes advantageous when it is paired with a mechanism that
checks what the command actually did, which is precisely the role a reversibility gate
and an undo record play in the present design.



Scarcity of comparative user evaluation
         A fourth pattern concerns evaluation method. Most surveyed systems are
evaluated either through architectural demonstration [11, 41] or through curated task
benchmarks [8, 42, 46, 53]. User studies are rare, and the comparisons that do exist are
narrow. VoicePilot [30] conducts a substantive human-centered evaluation of LLM-

based interaction, and AXIS [23] compares an API-first agent against unaided manual
operation in a within-subjects design — but within a single application, on a single
platform, with a cloud-hosted frontier model.        No surveyed work compares a
conversational interface against the conventional graphical workflow across operating-
system-level tasks on the same population. This is the empirical gap best filled by a
controlled study of conversational interfaces.



The operating-system substrate
         A fifth pattern concerns the operating-system substrate. Windows [15, 50],
Android [33, 42, 51], and the web [8, 52, 53] are each heavily represented; the Linux
work that exists is aimed elsewhere than the end user’s desktop session. NaSh [11]
addresses Linux at the shell level as a design proposal; Alibaba Cloud’s ANOLISA
ships a natural-language Linux terminal with approval gating and rollback, but scoped
to server and DevOps workloads; and OSWorld [46] includes Ubuntu as an evaluation
platform rather than a deployment target.        No surveyed system implements a

conversational session layer for the Linux desktop addressed to the person using the
machine, despite Linux serving as the substrate for most developer and server-side
workflows.

                                                                                      42


Shared lineage and its fragility
        A sixth pattern is the field’s shared intellectual lineage and its attendant
fragility. As established in §2.3, nearly every post-2023 system inherits the ReAct [47]
convention of interleaving reasoning with action, which explains both the field’s rapid

progress and the pervasiveness of LLM-reasoning failure modes across it — a fragility
that makes safety and recoverability mechanisms, rather than raw capability alone,
decisive for any deployable system.
        A related but distinct class of contemporary systems warrants explicit
positioning here: AI-assisted coding agents such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Aider,
which translate natural-language intent into shell commands and execute them under
human oversight — the same basic mechanism the present study adopts. These tools
are excluded from Table 2.2 not on the grounds of being industry rather than academic
work — a substantial share of the literature surveyed here is likewise unrefereed
preprint — but because they occupy a different position on every axis that matters to
this study. They are built for software engineers operating within an existing code
repository, are powered by large models hosted on remote infrastructure, and run as a
tool installed atop an otherwise unmodified desktop session. SynapseOS targets the
inverse on each axis: any user of the machine regardless of command-line fluency, a
locally-hosted small language model rather than a cloud dependency, and replacement
of the session layer itself rather than an application running within it. The mechanism
is shared; the population, the model’s location, and the system’s place in the stack are
not.
        These patterns converge on a fourfold research gap. First, every surveyed
system that guards a generated command does so by permission, matching it against an
enumerated list of dangerous patterns, and none determines what the command would

destroy or captures what is needed to undo it (§2.5); this is the gap the study’s
algorithmic contribution addresses. Second, no system in the literature provides a
conversational interface as a general route to operating a personal computer — one
addressed to the person using the machine, spanning the operating system rather than a

──────── p. 43 ────────
                                                                                      43


single application, and evaluated as an interface rather than as an autonomous agent.
Existing systems are automation utilities: they act on the user’s behalf, and they are
measured by how often they complete a task unattended rather than by whether a
person directs them more effectively than they direct the interfaces they already have.
Third, the work that exists on an open platform targets servers, agents, and benchmarks
rather than the desktop session of an end user — and the platform constraint runs
deeper than coverage. An interface layer that observes and acts on a desktop session
can only be built where the platform permits it; Windows and macOS are proprietary
and do not. Linux is therefore the condition of the experiment rather than its subject,
and the absence of end-user desktop work there is what this study addresses. Fourth,
controlled comparison against the conventional graphical workflow exists only within a
single application, on a single platform, with a cloud-hosted frontier model [23]; no
such comparison exists for operating-system-level tasks under a locally-hosted model
and an explicit safety gate. SynapseOS — a conversational interface layer for the
Linux desktop that translates natural-language intent into shell commands under a

reversibility-based confirmation gate, evaluated against each participant’s own
operating system — is positioned precisely against these four gaps. Its relationship to
the closest prior work is worth stating directly. NaSh [11] proposes guardrails for an
LLM-powered natural language shell — generated code that remains analyzable,
selective undo, summaries of what a command would affect — as a design position,
and explicitly leaves implementation and empirical evaluation as open work. The
present study is an implementation of that position, substituting for the general
principle a static recoverability analysis, which derives what a command would affect
from its structure before it runs, and an undo journal with five mechanisms, and
supplying the user evaluation NaSh defers.

2.10 Review of the Methodological Literature
        The algorithm is evaluated against ground truth observed by sandboxed
execution, with paired statistical tests, as Chapter 3, Section 3.1 describes.       The
methodological literature reviewed here shapes the validation study, in which the
analysis is deployed in an interface and exercised by people, and so concerns how a

conversational interface should be evaluated. The evaluation practices of the surveyed
work fall into three categories, each with a characteristic limitation. Architectural
demonstrations [11, 41] establish feasibility but provide no performance evidence.
Curated task benchmarks [8, 46, 53] provide reproducible, quantitative measures but
assess the autonomous task completion of agents acting on a user’s behalf rather than
the performance of a human working through an interface. Human-centered user
studies, exemplified by VoicePilot [30] and AXIS [23], provide the most direct
evidence of an interface’s value to real users but remain rare in this literature and,
where present, are confined to a single application rather than the operating system as a
whole. The instruments this study adopts are drawn from established practice rather
than constructed for it: the System Usability Scale [3] for perceived usability, with its
interpretation grounded in subsequent large-sample analysis [36], and the NASA Task
Load Index [13] for perceived workload.
        These limitations motivate the methodological posture of the present study,
which is grounded in established human-computer-interaction evaluation practice. The
study adopts a within-subjects design, in which each participant completes tasks under
both the SynapseOS condition and the condition of their own operating system, so that
each participant serves as their own control and between-person variance is removed as
a confound; the order of conditions is counterbalanced to distribute learning effects
evenly. It further adopts an expert-baseline comparison — using each participant’s
most familiar environment as the comparison condition rather than a fixed alternative

that may be unfamiliar — so that observed differences reflect interface quality rather
than differential familiarity with the operating system, strengthening the ecological
validity that the benchmark-only evaluations in the surveyed literature lack.         To

──────── p. 45 ────────
                                                                                        45


integrate the quantitative measures of task completion time and error rate with
qualitative evidence of user experience, the study employs a convergent mixed-methods
structure in the sense set out by Creswell and Plano Clark [7], collecting both strands
concurrently and integrating them at the interpretation stage; the qualitative data,
gathered through retrospective think-aloud over the screen recording and a semi-
structured interview, are analyzed using the thematic-analysis method of Braun and
Clarke [2], a widely adopted six-phase approach for identifying and reporting patterns
in qualitative data. Design considerations surfaced by the reviewed systems inform the
evaluation directly: the preview-and-confirm guideline of VoicePilot [30] and the
reversibility-and-guardrail framing of NaSh [11] together motivate an explicit
validation of the confirmation gate and undo mechanism as part of the study.
         The specific instantiation of this methodology — the composition of the
benchmark and custom task suite, the participant profile and screening criteria, the
session procedure, the metrics and their computation, and the statistical and qualitative
analysis plan — is presented in detail in Chapter 3.



2.11 Summary
         This chapter reviewed two literatures.        The first, on which the study’s
algorithm builds, is program analysis: abstract interpretation and type-and-effect
systems [6, 18, 40] supply its formal basis, the command guards of natural-language
shells and coding agents [11, 19, 29] decide by permission rather than by effect, the
static analyses of shell programs [1, 10, 25] target properties adjacent to recoverability
but not recoverability itself, and recovery mechanisms from snapshots to operating
system transactions [4, 9, 32, 34, 35] restore after the fact, but wholesale or only on a
system built for it. The second, in which the platform is situated, is the literature on
natural-language and LLM-mediated interfaces to personal computing across nearly
four decades, organized along a four-layer pipeline of Input, Reasoning, Action, and
Operating-System Integration. The historical foundations established the long-standing

──────── p. 46 ────────
                                                                                        46


aspiration to a natural-language interface to a real operating system [45] and the
methodological shift, driven by in-context reasoning [47], that made the current
generation of systems possible. The shell-and-command-line thread most directly
related to the proposed design demonstrated that natural-language-to-Bash translation
is tractable [43], that safety and reversibility are central concerns [11], and that local
deployment is feasible [17], while leaving the extension from shell to session, and

empirical user evaluation, as open work. The graphical-agent literature across web,
mobile, and desktop [8, 14, 15, 27, 33, 42, 50–53] established the field’s architectural
patterns and its recurring bottleneck of visual grounding, while the accessibility thread
[12, 26, 30, 31, 41] revealed a complementary limitation that a general conversational
interface layer would resolve. The evaluation literature [46, 48, 49] traced a capability
ceiling that has risen sharply on short tasks while remaining low on long-horizon work,
and exposed a methodological gap: interfaces are seldom evaluated comparatively
against the conventional workflow with real users, and never yet at the level of the
operating system itself.
         Synthesized, the surveyed works reveal a fourfold gap: no analysis of what a
generated command would destroy, every guard being a list of dangerous patterns; no
conversational replacement for the graphical session layer; no coverage of the Linux
desktop; and no controlled comparison of a conversational interface against the
conventional workflow across user populations. The first is the gap the present study’s
research addresses, through a recoverability analysis of generated shell commands
evaluated against ground truth obtained by sandboxed execution. The other three
describe the setting in which that gap arises, and SynapseOS, the conversational
interface layer for the Linux desktop built as the analysis’s experimental platform, is
where the analysis is deployed and validated in use, through a controlled, within-
subjects comparison against each participant’s own operating system.                  The
methodology through which this evaluation is conducted is the subject of Chapter 3.
